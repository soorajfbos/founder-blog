## Run a Quarterly Planning Session That Actually Works

Weekly planning keeps you busy. Quarterly planning keeps you pointed in the right direction. A short, well run session twice a year or every quarter can save months of drifting work.

### Prepare Before the Meeting

Spend two hours gathering facts first:
- What shipped last quarter and what slipped
- Your five key metrics and how they moved
- Top customer requests by count and revenue
- Top technical risks and debt items
- Current burn and months of runway
- Team capacity for the next quarter

Share this with the team three days early. Planning goes badly when people see the data for the first time in the room.

### Start by Looking Back

Spend the first 30 minutes on honest review:
- Which goals did we hit?
- Which did we miss and why?
- What surprised us?
- What did we learn about our customers?

No blame. Just facts. Teams that skip this step repeat the same mistakes.

### Pick One Main Goal

Choose a single outcome for the quarter. One, not five. Examples:
- Raise activation from 25 to 40 percent
- Reach 100 paying teams
- Cut churn in half for small customers
- Reach reliability good enough to close enterprise deals

Everything else supports this. If a project does not help, it waits.

### Add Two or Three Supporting Bets

Under the main goal, list the bets you will make. For each one write:
- The problem
- Who it affects
- What success looks like in a number
- A rough size in weeks
- The biggest risk

Keep each bet to one page. If it needs more, it is too big for one quarter.

### Count Real Capacity

Be honest about time:
- Count maker days, not calendar days
- Subtract holidays, interviews, and support duty
- Reserve 20 percent for technical health
- Reserve 10 percent for customer requests and fixes

What remains is what you can actually plan. Most teams plan double this and then feel like failures.

### Write Down What You Will Not Do

This list is as important as the plan. Name five things you are choosing not to build this quarter and say why. When someone raises them later, point at the list.

Publishing this prevents quiet scope creep and endless debate.

### Name Owners and Check Dates

Every bet needs:
- One owner, not a committee
- A date for a midpoint check
- A clear definition of done

The midpoint check is where you cut scope if you are behind. Put it in the calendar during the planning meeting.

### Keep the Plan on One Page

The whole quarter should fit on a single page:
- The main goal
- Two or three bets with owners
- Key metrics you are watching
- What you are not doing

Pin it where everyone sees it. A plan nobody can remember is not a plan.

### Review Monthly, Not Just at the End

Hold a 30 minute monthly check:
- Are the metrics moving?
- Is each bet on track?
- Does anything need cutting or swapping?

Changing the plan with reasons is good. Changing it silently every week is not.

### Close the Quarter Properly

In the last week, write a short summary:
- What we set out to do
- What we shipped
- What the numbers did
- What we learned
- What we will carry forward

Share it with the whole team and your investors. This habit builds trust and makes the next planning session much easier.

### Keep It Short

The whole planning session should take two to three hours. If it takes a full day, you are deciding too much detail. Set direction in the room. Work out details afterward.
