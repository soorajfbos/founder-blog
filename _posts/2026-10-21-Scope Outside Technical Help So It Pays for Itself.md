## Scope Outside Technical Help So It Pays for Itself

Bringing in senior technical help from outside works well when it is scoped tightly and ends cleanly. It goes badly when the brief is vague, the work never transfers to your team, and you end up dependent on someone who is not there every day.

### Decide What Problem You Are Buying

Before talking to anyone, write the problem in one paragraph. Common ones:
- We cannot tell whether our system will survive growth
- Delivery has slowed and we do not know why
- We need to hire engineers and nobody can assess them
- A large customer wants security assurances we cannot give
- Our agency is leaving and we need to take the code in house

If you cannot write the paragraph, you are not ready to engage anyone. Vague briefs produce vague invoices.

### Define Three or Four Outcomes

Turn the problem into deliverables you can check:
- A written risk assessment with a ranked fix list
- A twelve month technical plan agreed with the team
- A hiring scorecard and two completed technical interviews
- A security documentation pack ready to send to buyers
- A deploy pipeline our team can run without help

Each outcome should be something you can hold, read, or run. Not "advise on strategy."

### Set the Time Box Up Front

Most useful engagements fit in six to twelve weeks. Longer than that and either the scope was wrong or the work should be done by an employee.

Agree the end date at the start. Open ended arrangements drift into expensive dependency.

### Keep Your Team Involved From Day One

The point is to leave your team stronger, not to have work done to them. Insist on:
- Pairing on anything technical rather than solo work
- Your engineers in every planning session
- Documentation written in your repository, not sent as attachments
- Decisions recorded with reasons so they survive the engagement

If knowledge leaves when the engagement ends, you bought nothing durable.

### Protect Access and Ownership

Standard rules apply:
- Accounts created by you, access granted not transferred
- All work in your repositories from day one
- A signed agreement covering confidentiality and ownership
- Access reviewed and removed on the final day

### Agree How You Will Judge It

Pick measures before starting. For example:
- Delivery time from task start to production falls by a third
- All critical risks have an owner and a date
- Two engineers can run a deploy and a rollback unaided
- The security pack answers a real customer questionnaire

Review these at the halfway point, not at the end. Halfway is when you can still change course.

### Watch for Warning Signs

Act early if you see:
- Work happening without your team present
- Documents delivered that nobody on your side can maintain
- Scope expanding without a written change note
- Recommendations that would require continued involvement to execute
- No clear answer to what finished looks like

### Plan the Handover From the Start

Write the handover requirements into the first agreement:
- Everything documented in your systems
- A walkthrough session recorded
- A list of what remains and who owns it
- Two weeks of light availability for questions afterwards

A clean ending is a sign the engagement worked.

### Know When an Employee Is the Right Answer

If the need is ongoing daily leadership, team management, and long term ownership, hire. Outside help suits defined problems: assessments, setting standards, unblocking a specific situation, bridging a gap while you search.

Spending sensibly on [outsourced cto services](https://foundersbar.com/articles-and-research/top-strategies-for-effective-startup-software-development) means buying judgement for a defined period, then owning the result yourself.

### The Test

At the end, your team should be able to maintain everything that was built or decided, without calling anyone. If that is true, the money was well spent.
